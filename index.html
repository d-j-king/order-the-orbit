Order the Orbit

a sim for poking at dⁿx/dtⁿ = −kx
launch playground →

The usual mass-on-a-spring ties acceleration to displacement: \(\ddot{x} = -kx\). Substitute a higher derivative—jerk, snap, crackle—and the eigenvalue picture changes completely; only one order gives stable orbits.

A second mode: keep it second-order but let the restoring force grow nonlinearly with displacement. The period then depends on amplitude, and orbits precess into rosettes.

Derivative tower
\[ \frac{d^n x}{dt^n} = -k\,x \]
n = order of the derivative tied to displacement. n = 2 is the only stable case.
Power law spring
\[ F = -k\,x\left|\frac{x}{R}\right|^{n-1} \]
Second-order, nonlinear. n = 1 is normal SHM; n > 1 gives amplitude-dependent precession.
things to notice
  • n = 2 is the unique stable case in the derivative tower. The eigenvalues of \(r^n = -k\) land on the imaginary axis only when n = 2; every other order has at least one root with positive real part.
  • Drag velocity sets the initial kick. A slow release gives radial SHM. A fast flick at an angle launches an orbit. The arrow shown during drag is the velocity vector.
  • Power law n = 1 is physically identical to derivative n = 2—same ODE, same eigenvalues, same orbits. The interesting regime starts at n = 2 where orbits begin to precess.
  • The sidebar diagram updates live—eigenvalue positions or potential well shape—as you change n or k.
  • For n ≥ 3 in the derivative tower, bodies escape in finite time. Any eigenvalue with \(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda) > 0\) causes exponential growth regardless of initial conditions.
